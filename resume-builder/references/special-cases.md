# Special cases

## Students with little or no experience
- Education goes first. Add 3 to 5 relevant courses (completed or enrolled; mark upcoming ones as "Upcoming").
- Projects carry the weight: class, personal, competition, volunteer and open-source work all count if there is a real problem, method and result.
- Part-time jobs, family business, volunteering, clubs and sports count when you show what you did and what changed. "Served 80 customers a shift" is a number too.
- Do not pad with "team player". Show one story instead.

## Career changers
- Lead with a short summary that names the new direction and the proof ("Accountant moving into data analysis; built X in SQL").
- Put a Skills block and Projects ahead of old experience, then translate old duties into the new language only where it is true.
- Say in the needs-input file which postings would see the career change as a gap and how to speak to it.

## Gaps in employment
- Do not hide them with invented work. A short honest line works ("Career break: caring for family, 2023 to 2024") or dates by year only. Ask the person which they prefer.
- Any learning during the gap (courses, projects) can go in Projects with dates.

## International students and newcomers
- Use "City, Country" for every location, including work done abroad. Do not translate or inflate foreign job titles; describe the work plainly.
- Eligibility (work permit, school co-op letter, graduation window) belongs in `needs-input.md`, not on the resume. Flag it for every posting because many portals ask.
- Mention languages with an honest level.

## Experienced people
- Experience first. Keep the last 10 to 15 years; compress older roles to one line.
- Choose 3 to 5 achievements with numbers per recent role. Drop duties that anyone in the role would have.

## Founders and self-employed
- Give the business a one-line description and your real title. Numbers must be ones you can show from records. If you sold or closed the business, state it factually; ask the person whether to include why.

## Creative or academic CVs
- Many countries use longer CVs for academia, medicine or some European jobs. If the person says one page is not the norm, follow their norm and tell them what changes.

## Thin profiles (one project, one volunteer role, no paid work)
1. Ask three follow-ups before drafting: what the project does and who it is for, what exactly they did in the volunteer role (and how many people or how often), and what they would show an interviewer.
2. Target 2 bullets per item. Accept a short resume. Do not pad (see writing-rules.md).
3. Do not stretch a project into a match for the posting ("close to Python scripting"). Name the real overlap and leave the rest to the interview.
4. Check the student year against the dates and ask if they differ.

## Employment gaps (person chooses)
- Options: show years only, add a one-line honest explanation, or fill it with real learning (courses, projects with dates). Ask which they prefer and record it.
- If they leave the gap off, write a one-sentence spoken answer in `prep.md` so they are not surprised in an interview.

## Work authorization wording
Never decide or state eligibility for them and do not give legal advice. Record the facts they give (status, permit dates) in `needs-input.md` as a private flag, and tell them to answer each portal's question truthfully with the exact wording the portal asks for. Do not put permit status on the resume unless they ask.

## Academics moving to industry
- **CV versus resume is a soft rule.** Explain once: industry hiring teams read a one-page resume with 2 to 3 selected publications; the full publication list belongs in a separate academic CV. Offer both: `First_Last_Resume_<Company>.pdf` (one page) and `First_Last_AcademicCV.pdf` (build with `--max-pages 3`). Do not pad the CV to reach a page count.
- Ask for publication details: title, venue, year, author position. Do not invent a venue or year.
- Translate research into industry words only where true: "clustered gene expression data" becomes "clustering analysis on a dataset of N samples" only if N is given. Ask for dataset size, tools and what decision the work informed.
- Do not call the person by a title the posting wants ("Data Scientist") in the summary; say what they did and what they are seeking.
- Ask about deployment: "Did you ever run code in production, or only in research?" Record the honest answer.

## Contracts and unknown end dates
Write "Jul 2025 to present (contract, ends Dec 2026)" if they know the end; otherwise "to present". Ask; do not guess.

## Management targets
Ask scope before writing any leadership claim: how many people (direct reports vs. team), budget (set it, track it, or only see a report), and what they decided themselves. A person who only sees a budget report did not manage the budget; write what they did.

## Certifications and licences
Give them a CERTIFICATIONS section (name, year, expiry). Check expiry dates against today and ask if anything has lapsed.

## Non-English resumes
Use the same fact sheet and the same numbers. Translate headings and use accents and local conventions the person prefers. The checker's banned-word list is English only, so read the Spanish, French or other text yourself for empty buzzwords. Keep file names ASCII (no accents or spaces) so portals accept them. Use CEFR-style language levels when the person knows them (B2 = upper-intermediate), never "fluent" for B2 or lower.
